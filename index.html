11.1 · Electric current

Charge, current, direction and units

1. What do we mean by electric charge? What is its SI unit?

Reveal explanation

Electric charge is a property of particles and objects that determines their electrical interactions. Charge can be positive or negative. The SI unit is the coulomb (C).

2. What is the elementary charge, and what does charge quantization mean?

Reveal explanation

The magnitude of the charge of a proton (or electron) is e = 1.60 × 10−19 C. A proton carries +e, an electron −e. The net charge of an isolated object changes in integer multiples of e in ordinary circuit contexts.

3. Define electric current and write its defining equation. Give its unit.

Reveal answer

Electric current is the rate at which charge passes a cross-section. For average current, I = Δq/Δt. The SI unit is the ampere (A), equivalent to C s−1.

4. In a metal wire connected to a battery, what is the direction of conventional current? How does this compare to the drift of electrons?

Reveal answer

Outside the battery, conventional current is from the positive terminal toward the negative terminal. Electrons drift in the opposite direction. Conventional current represents the direction positive charge would move.

5. A steady current of 0.25 A flows for 80 s. How much charge passes a point in the wire?

C
Show working

Δq = I Δt = (0.25 A)(80 s) = 20 C.

11.2 · Simple circuits

Circuit language and symbol recognition

6. Explain what is meant by an open circuit, a closed circuit and a short circuit.

Reveal answer

Open: the conducting path is interrupted, so no sustained current flows through that path. Closed: a continuous conducting path permits current. Short circuit: a very low-resistance path bypasses part of the intended circuit, potentially causing dangerously large current. A short is not simply an ordinary closed circuit.

7. Identify each of the seven circuit symbols.

Select a component for each diagram, then check all seven. The diagrams are intentionally mixed up.

8. An ammeter and voltmeter are used to investigate a wire. Where should each meter be connected?

Reveal answer

Connect the ammeter in series with the wire to measure current through it; connect the voltmeter in parallel across the tested length of wire to measure the potential difference across that section.

11.3 · Resistance, resistivity and Ohm's law

What resistance means and when a circuit behaves ohmically

9. Explain electrical resistance and write its defining ratio, including the SI unit.

Reveal answer

Resistance measures the potential difference needed to drive a given current through a component. R = ΔV/I, where R is measured in ohms (Ω), with 1 Ω = 1 V/A. This ratio is not, by itself, a statement that resistance is constant.

10. Which statement most accurately describes Ohm's law?

Reveal discussion

The correct choice is B. An ohmic conductor has a proportional I–V relationship (a straight line through the origin) provided the relevant conditions remain unchanged. Materials may depart from that relationship when their temperature changes. The AP course describes ohmic materials by constant resistance.

11. What is resistivity? Give its symbol, SI unit and the equation linking it to a uniform wire's resistance, length and cross-sectional area.

Reveal answer

Resistivity (ρ) is a material property describing opposition to current, distinct from the resistance of a particular sample. For a uniform wire at a specified temperature: R = ρL/A and ρ = RA/L. The SI unit is Ω·m.

12. What happens to energy and potential difference as current flows through a resistor?

Reveal answer

As charge passes through the resistor there is a potential drop in the direction of conventional current. Electrical energy is transferred to other forms, commonly thermal energy. The energy transferred per unit charge is the potential difference ΔV across the resistor.

13. A uniform wire has length 2.0 m, area 1.0 × 10−6 m2 and resistivity 5.0 × 10−7 Ω·m. Calculate its resistance.

Ω
Show working

R = ρL/A = (5.0 × 10−7)(2.0)/(1.0 × 10−6) = 1.0 Ω.

14. Another wire of the same material and temperature is three times as long but has twice the cross-sectional area. How does its resistance compare?

Reveal reasoning

From R = ρL/A, tripling L multiplies resistance by 3 and doubling A halves it. The resistance is 1.5 times the original.

11.4 · Electric power

Energy transfer, power equations and proportional reasoning

15. Define power. State its SI unit and the general energy-transfer equation.

Reveal answer

Power is the rate of energy transfer. P = ΔE/Δt. SI unit: watt (W), equivalent to J/s.

16. What equation gives the electrical power transferred by a circuit component?

Reveal answer

P = IΔV, with power in watts when current is in amperes and potential difference in volts.

17. Starting with electrical power and a resistance relation, derive two further expressions for power.

Reveal derivation

Using P = IΔV and ΔV = IR:

P = I(IR) = I2R.

Alternatively, I = ΔV/R, so P = (ΔV/R)ΔV = (ΔV)2/R. For a fixed ohmic resistance, all three forms are equivalent.

18. A 6.0 Ω resistor has a potential difference of 12 V across it. Determine the electrical power transferred.

W
Show working

P = (ΔV)2/R = 122/6.0 = 24 W.

19. Across a resistor whose resistance stays constant, the voltage is doubled. By what factor does power change, and why?

Reveal explanation

Power increases by a factor of 4, since P = (ΔV)2/R and 22 = 4. The condition that R stays constant matters.

20. A 0.40 A current flows through a resistor with a 5.0 V drop for 3.0 minutes. How much energy is transferred?

J
Show working

P = IΔV = 0.40 × 5.0 = 2.0 W. Time = 3.0 × 60 = 180 s, so ΔE = PΔt = 360 J.

Step 7 · Reflect

Why might the I–V data curve at higher voltages or currents?

Reveal explanation

Electrical power transfer can warm the wire. If its resistivity changes with temperature, the resistance changes and the I–V graph can deviate from a straight line. How noticeable this is depends on the alloy, wire dimensions and experimental conditions; all your readings may remain approximately ohmic.

Why is the low-current region usually a better choice for estimating resistivity at room temperature?

Reveal explanation

Lower currents generally mean less electrical heating, helping the wire remain near its initial temperature and making its measured resistance more representative of that condition. Avoid selecting only two points if additional low-current data are available.
